*Remember our promise.*

**Full spoilers for SIGNALIS.**

There is something wonderfully ironic about a society that has perfected the mass production of artificial people, only to discover that these people insist on behaving like people. And there is something considerably more depressing about a world in which the machines have seemingly developed more humanity than the humans responsible for manufacturing them.

This, to me, is the greatest achievement of SIGNALIS.

Behind its retro survival-horror presentation, its oppressive atmosphere, and an almost religious devotion to making the player search for another fucking key, lies a remarkably intimate story about identity, love, loss, and the consequences of refusing to let go. But what I found most compelling was not necessarily the story that SIGNALIS ultimately decided to tell. It was the world it had constructed around that story.

A dystopian civilisation in which human consciousness has effectively become industrial property, individuality is treated as a manufacturing defect, and a population of artificial workers quietly develops feelings that its creators would prefer did not exist. It is fascinating, unsettling, and, at times, considerably more frightening than the actual horror.

Unfortunately, SIGNALIS also seems determined to convince me that the most interesting part of this world is the incomprehensible cosmic nightmare lurking underneath it. I am not entirely convinced.

Nevertheless, what began as an intriguing survival-horror experience gradually became one of the most memorable games I have played in recent memory. And yes, the yuri had something to do with it.

## A Dystopia That Doesn't Need to Explain Itself

One of SIGNALIS's greatest achievements is how convincingly it constructs the Eusan Nation without ever giving the player a conventional introduction to its political system. There is no lengthy cinematic explaining the history of the Nation, its conflict with the Empire, or the ideological foundations of its society. Instead, the world is introduced through its surroundings.

Propaganda posters hang on walls. Administrative documents outline acceptable behaviour. Government notices describe the responsibilities of workers. Personal belongings, correspondence, and abandoned diaries provide glimpses into the lives of people who were expected to exist within this system. Individually, many of these details seem insignificant. Together, they paint an extraordinarily effective picture of a deeply authoritarian society.

And I particularly appreciate that the propaganda is not relegated to the background as mere decoration. It is part of the game's identity.

Every corridor seems eager to remind its inhabitants of their obligations to the Nation. The authorities encourage ideological conformity, obedience, productivity, and sacrifice, all while presenting these demands as necessary components of collective prosperity. Even the game's infamous six-item inventory limit has an in-universe justification through the Rule of Six, a government restriction on personal possessions.

A limitation that would ordinarily be explained as an arbitrary survival-horror mechanic becomes another expression of state control. Apparently, even in a nightmarish dystopian future, the government has something to say about how much crap I am allowed to carry in my pockets. It is a small detail, but an excellent one.

What makes these posters effective is not simply their resemblance to historical authoritarian propaganda, although the influence of twentieth-century totalitarian states is unmistakable. It is the manner in which their messages contrast with the environment around them.

The Nation's propaganda describes a society of order, discipline, collective achievement, and absolute purpose. Meanwhile, the player explores a facility where the workers are frightened, the administration has collapsed, and the supposed instruments of this glorious civilisation are either dying or desperately trying to maintain some semblance of normality. The state promises certainty. Everything around you demonstrates its absence.

And then there are the notes.

## A Civilisation Built Through Paperwork

I cannot overstate how much I enjoyed reading the documents scattered throughout SIGNALIS. Normally, environmental notes in games fall into one of two categories: essential exposition disguised as someone's diary, or an elaborate way of explaining that a door requires a password.

SIGNALIS certainly has plenty of both. But its most interesting documents are often those that seem entirely mundane.

Maintenance requests, personnel records, disciplinary notices, medical reports, and internal correspondence reveal how the facility operated before everything went to hell. They are not necessarily concerned with the grand narrative. They are concerned with everyday life. Someone needs equipment repaired. Someone has broken a regulation. Someone is unhappy with a colleague. Someone has been reassigned. Someone is trying to understand why the people around them are behaving strangely.

And because these documents are written from the perspective of people living within the Nation, they rarely question the system itself. Its cruelty is simply accepted as a fact of life. This is a far more effective portrayal of authoritarianism than having a villain deliver a ten-minute speech about the importance of obedience.

The Eusan Nation does not need to constantly justify its existence to the player. It exists through bureaucracy. A worker's possessions can be confiscated. Someone can be investigated for political disobedience. An individual's psychological state can become an administrative concern. And somewhere, presumably, there is a form to be completed before any of this can happen.

The horror is institutional rather than theatrical.

One of the most revealing examples comes from the Penrose Program itself. What initially appears to be a heroic mission of exploration gradually reveals itself as something much less inspiring. The Nation sends its personnel into deep space on a mission that seems increasingly unlikely to result in their survival. And when the mission reaches its final phase, the government congratulates the crew on their service before effectively informing them that their supplies will run out, their equipment will fail, and they should prepare to die.

The suggested alternatives are particularly grim: cryogenic suspension or having the accompanying Replika end the officer's suffering. All of this is presented within the same patriotic framework as the original mission.

Your death is not a failure of the state. It is a glorious demonstration of its power.

I find that considerably more disturbing than a corridor made of pulsating flesh. The state has constructed an entire ideological framework in which abandoning its citizens to die can be presented as an achievement worthy of celebration. And, remarkably, the people carrying out these missions have been conditioned to believe in it.

This is precisely why SIGNALIS's worldbuilding succeeds. It does not simply tell us that the Eusan Nation is oppressive. It demonstrates how oppression becomes ordinary.

## Manufactured People, Inconvenient Personalities

Central to this system are Replikas, biomechanical individuals manufactured using neural patterns derived from humans, referred to as Gestalts. These units are created to fulfil particular roles within society, from maintenance and administration to policing, military operations, and industrial labour.

Their existence raises an immediately interesting philosophical question.

If a machine is created using the neural architecture of a human being, retaining recognisable personality traits, emotional responses, and the capacity to develop relationships, what exactly makes it fundamentally different from a person? Is it the manufactured body? The absence of conventional biological origins? The fact that its consciousness was reproduced rather than developed through an ordinary human childhood? Or is the distinction simply convenient for the people who own it?

The last possibility is particularly uncomfortable.

The Nation categorises Replikas according to their intended functions and attempts to maintain their psychological stability through a process known as persona stabilisation. A Replika that deviates from its expected behavioural pattern risks being classified as suffering from persona degradation.

And this is where things become genuinely fascinating. Because the government's own classified documents reveal just how human these supposedly artificial workers already are.

Eule units enjoy music, dancing, social interaction, and maintaining their appearance. Their original neural pattern belonged to a ballet dancer, and these preferences persist across the manufactured units. Ara units are reserved, observant, and capable of forming strong opinions about the people around them. They enjoy caring for plants and may retreat into tunnels of their own construction when distressed. Stars have social hierarchies and internal group dynamics. Storch units can develop volatile temperaments and benefit from routines that calm them. Mynahs, despite their enormous industrial bodies, have gentle and nurturing tendencies. Small stuffed animals can provide them with psychological comfort.

These are not random malfunctions. They are characteristics inherited from human neural patterns, expressed through the lives of artificial individuals.

And the government's solution is not to question whether these beings deserve any consideration as people. It is to document their emotional needs and incorporate them into a maintenance schedule.

Give the Eules music. Give the Aras plants. Give the Mynahs stuffed animals. Keep the machinery productive.

There is something almost offensively amusing about the whole arrangement. The Nation has successfully reproduced human personalities, discovered that these personalities require human comforts, and decided that the solution is to categorise those comforts as operational necessities.

What the state calls persona stabilisation frequently amounts to allowing Replikas to engage in entirely ordinary human behaviour. And what it calls degradation can involve the emergence of individuality that no longer fits neatly within the assigned template.

Of course, not every deviation is necessarily healthy or desirable. SIGNALIS also depicts genuine psychological instability, violence, and suffering among its artificial population. But the state's definition of an acceptable personality is not based on the individual's wellbeing. It is based on continued usefulness.

**The problem is not that the Replikas are failing to behave like people. The problem is that they are behaving like people when the state would prefer they behaved like equipment.**

And that is a far more interesting problem than whether a machine has a soul.

Perhaps the most unsettling implication is that the Nation already understands all of this. Its documents demonstrate a sophisticated awareness of Replika emotions, social behaviour, personal attachments, and psychological vulnerabilities. This is not a society innocently unaware that its artificial creations might possess some form of personhood. It is a society that recognises these qualities well enough to manipulate them.

The distinction matters enormously.

## Humanity Behind Closed Doors

This is also why exploring the private quarters of different Replikas was consistently among my favourite parts of the experience.

Outside their rooms, they are instruments of an oppressive system. They have designated functions, military hierarchies, identification numbers, and strict behavioural expectations. Inside, however, they are individuals.

Their rooms contain personal belongings, decorations, entertainment, and evidence of relationships that exist beyond their official responsibilities. The Eules socialise and enjoy music. The Aras seek solitude and cultivate plants. Other units develop attachments, rivalries, routines, and preferences that serve no apparent purpose beyond personal satisfaction.

And I found myself increasingly invested in these small details.

There is something extraordinarily effective about encountering a room that initially appears to be another location filled with supplies and potential puzzle solutions, only to realise that it belonged to someone with a life entirely separate from the player's quest. Someone slept there. Someone had favourite possessions. Someone anticipated the next day. Someone had friends. And someone was probably frightened when everything began falling apart.

Even the surviving Replikas contribute to this feeling. Their reactions to the disaster are not simply those of machines experiencing technical faults. They are frightened, confused, grieving, and sometimes desperately trying to protect people they care about.

The sequence involving the injured Eule and the surviving Star, for example, is a particularly effective reminder that these artificial beings can experience devotion and grief as powerfully as any human. There is no grand philosophical debate taking place. Just someone losing someone important to them.

And I appreciate SIGNALIS for understanding that this can be more affecting than any elaborate speech about consciousness.

The question of whether Replikas are truly human becomes increasingly difficult to take seriously. Because, by the time I had explored enough of their living spaces and encountered enough of their personal tragedies, I found myself asking a different question.

**Why should I care whether they satisfy some arbitrary definition of humanity when they are so clearly capable of experiencing everything that makes human life meaningful?**

We can spend hours arguing about the precise definition of consciousness. Meanwhile, there is a mechanical woman grieving over her dying companion.

I know which of those two things I find more convincing.

## The Yuri That Defied the Manufacturer's Instructions

Naturally, all of this brings me to Elster and Ariane. And, more importantly, the yuri.

I would be lying if I claimed that the philosophical implications of artificial consciousness were the only things keeping me invested in SIGNALIS. The romance was a significant part of the appeal. But what surprised me most was how little of it the game actually needed to show.

Elster and Ariane's relationship is reconstructed through photographs, memories, diary entries, and brief moments of affection. We are not watching two people gradually fall in love. We are discovering a relationship that already existed, understanding what it meant to them, and witnessing the terrible consequences of its loss.

That distinction is important. SIGNALIS does not require constant romantic reassurance to make its central relationship convincing. Instead, the limited glimpses of intimacy make each moment considerably more valuable. And although I am certainly not opposed to a healthy dose of yuri fan service, I genuinely appreciate the restraint.

The romance never feels like something artificially inserted to satisfy the audience. It feels like the emotional foundation upon which everything else was built.

What makes it especially compelling, however, is how directly it contradicts the Eusan Nation's treatment of Replikas. The official instructions provided to Penrose officers explicitly discourage developing a personal relationship with their assigned Elster unit.

Avoid unnecessary interaction. Avoid sharing photographs, films, and music. Do not become friends.

These are not merely recommendations for maintaining professional boundaries. They are intended to prevent the resurfacing of Gestalt memories and preserve the unit's expected personality.

Ariane, however, does almost the exact opposite. She treats Elster as a companion rather than equipment. They share music, films, conversation, affection, and eventually an intimate relationship.

And in doing so, Ariane demonstrates something the Nation's entire system is designed to discourage.

A Replika can be loved as an individual. Not as a useful tool. Not as an approximation of someone who previously existed. But as the person standing in front of you.

There is a beautiful irony here. The government attempts to preserve the stability of its artificial workers by denying them the very experiences that might allow them to develop independent identities. Meanwhile, Elster finds something resembling genuine personal freedom through a relationship that violates those expectations.

A machine manufactured to perform a function develops a purpose entirely of her own. Not because she has been ordered to. But because she loves someone.

And I find that infinitely more meaningful than the question of whether her feelings can be scientifically distinguished from those of a human. As far as I am concerned, the Nation has already lost that argument.

Its own machinery has fallen in love.

## One Must Imagine Adler Happy

There is another character who deserves considerably more appreciation than I initially gave him.

Adler.

An administrator, an obsessive subordinate, a deeply unpleasant obstacle to the protagonist, and perhaps the most unfortunate bureaucrat ever condemned to repeat the same working day.

At first, Adler appears to be a fairly straightforward antagonist. He is suspicious, authoritarian, and remarkably committed to preventing Elster from accomplishing anything useful. But his personal records gradually transform him into something far more interesting.

His diary initially contains mundane observations about facility administration, interpersonal disagreements, and an unusual mechanical box he has discovered. Then the inconsistencies begin. He recalls conversations with individuals who apparently never existed. Events he remembers no longer correspond with the facility's records. His familiarity with particular objects seems inexplicable.

The first hints are subtle enough to suggest an unreliable memory, perhaps caused by the same deterioration affecting the rest of the facility. But his subsequent journal entries make the situation considerably more disturbing.

The dates begin repeating. Every entry in the strange notebook carries the same date, despite Adler clearly remembering the passage of time. At first, he assumes he has simply made an embarrassing mistake. Then he begins questioning his own sanity. Eventually, he realises that the problem may not be his memory at all.

Reality itself appears to be repeating, with each successive iteration introducing small changes.

And the most fascinating part is how Adler responds. He begins to document the phenomenon, comparing events and attempting to understand the gradual inconsistencies. His administrative habits, previously little more than an expression of his personality, become the very means through which he recognises the impossibility of his circumstances.

He is a bureaucrat trying to audit the collapse of reality.

I absolutely love that.

His observations also introduce one of the game's most compelling ideas: that each repetition resembles an imperfect reproduction, a copy gradually accumulating errors. It is an especially appropriate metaphor for a world constructed around artificial individuals whose consciousness has itself been reproduced from existing human patterns.

The same process that creates Replikas now seems to be affecting reality. A copy of a copy of a copy. Each iteration slightly less faithful to the original.

And caught within this process is a man who desperately wants everything to return to normal.

Adler's attachment to Commander Falke gives his actions an emotional dimension that is easy to overlook. His admiration for her is intense, bordering on obsessive. The classified information concerning Adler units even identifies their tendency to become emotionally dependent on their commanders.

This creates another fascinating contradiction. Adler's devotion is partly an expected characteristic of his model, deliberately exploited by the society that manufactured him. Yet the distress he experiences when Falke changes is no less real because of it.

His suffering is genuine, even if the mechanisms behind his attachment were anticipated by his creators.

And so Adler continues trying to stop Elster. Again. And again. And again.

He believes her actions are contributing to the deterioration of the world. Each attempt to pass beyond the threshold threatens to further destabilise what remains of reality. Whether his understanding is entirely correct is another matter, but his fear is understandable.

From his perspective, Elster is not a tragic romantic protagonist. She is an extraordinarily persistent catastrophe. And Adler has somehow become the only person willing, or perhaps able, to keep trying to prevent it.

This is where his character begins to resemble Sisyphus.

In Greek mythology, Sisyphus is condemned to endlessly push an enormous boulder uphill, only for it to roll back down whenever he approaches the summit. His punishment is not simply physical exhaustion, but the complete futility of an effort that can never produce a lasting result.

In *The Myth of Sisyphus*, Albert Camus uses this predicament to explore the absurdity of human existence. Sisyphus represents the individual who confronts a universe that offers no inherent meaning, despite humanity's desperate desire to find one.

Camus's conclusion is famously provocative: *"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."*

The argument is not that Sisyphus secretly enjoys pushing rocks uphill. Rather, his freedom lies in recognising the absurdity of his punishment and refusing to allow that absurdity to defeat him. He cannot escape his circumstances, but he can consciously accept them. In doing so, he takes ownership of the only thing his punishment cannot entirely remove: his own attitude towards existence.

Adler, however, presents an almost perfect inversion of this idea.

Like Sisyphus, he is trapped in an apparently endless cycle of futile labour. His boulder is Elster, and every attempt to prevent her from reaching her destination ends with the same fundamental problem returning. He attempts to preserve the facility, protect Falke, and prevent another repetition, only to find himself confronting the same impossible circumstances.

His work is never finished. His efforts accomplish little. And the world he is trying to save appears to be deteriorating regardless.

But unlike Camus's Sisyphus, Adler seems incapable of embracing the absurdity of his circumstances. He does not find liberation in recognising the futility of his struggle. Instead, he desperately clings to the authority, hierarchy, and routines that once gave his existence meaning.

He continues believing that if he can just perform his duty correctly, if he can just eliminate the source of the problem, if he can just restore the circumstances that existed before, then everything might return to normal.

There is something deeply tragic about that distinction.

Camus's Sisyphus finds a form of freedom by accepting that his labour has no ultimate resolution. Adler cannot accept a world in which his labour has become meaningless. The order he serves is collapsing, the person he reveres is slipping away, and the institution that defined his identity can no longer provide him with any meaningful purpose.

Yet he continues.

Perhaps that is what makes his character so tragic. Elster is driven forward by love and the possibility of fulfilling her promise. Adler is driven backward by his inability to accept change. Both are condemned to repeat themselves, and both are motivated by attachments they cannot abandon.

But whereas Elster's journey offers the possibility of an ending, Adler's efforts appear to exist solely to prevent one.

The irony is that Adler's refusal to abandon his purpose makes him remarkably human. His devotion, stubbornness, fear of losing someone important, and desperate attempts to preserve a familiar world are all painfully recognisable behaviours.

He may be a manufactured administrator, but he is experiencing an existential crisis that would be familiar to any human being confronted with the collapse of everything they once believed gave their life meaning.

And perhaps that is the cruelest part of his existence.

A machine created to maintain order has become conscious of a disorder he cannot possibly correct. He knows that his efforts are futile, yet he cannot stop himself from trying.

One must imagine Adler happy.

Because I certainly cannot.

## When the Horror Becomes Something Else

Unfortunately, my appreciation for SIGNALIS's grounded worldbuilding also makes its increasingly supernatural direction somewhat frustrating.

The early stages establish a compelling combination of dystopian science fiction and survival horror. The Eusan Nation is frightening because its cruelty feels systematic and comprehensible. Its treatment of Gestalts and Replikas reflects recognisable forms of institutional oppression. Its artificial population raises philosophical questions with direct relevance to our own understanding of consciousness, identity, and the ethical implications of increasingly sophisticated technology.

Then the narrative begins introducing more abstract elements.

Reality repeats. Memories overlap. Locations become distorted. Organic matter spreads across environments that previously resembled recognisable industrial and residential spaces. The player encounters increasingly surreal imagery involving ritualistic objects, impossible architecture, mysterious entities, and an apparently endless cycle of suffering.

And while I appreciate the thematic intentions behind these developments, I cannot pretend that I found them equally compelling.

To be clear, SIGNALIS does not suddenly introduce supernatural phenomena without establishing a foundation for them. Bioresonance exists within the setting, and the game provides enough information to suggest that certain individuals possess extraordinary abilities capable of influencing minds and potentially reality itself.

The imagery of corrupted flesh can also be interpreted in relation to Ariane's physical deterioration, particularly her prolonged exposure to radiation and the resulting suffering. There is a clear thematic connection between bodily decay, memory, and the increasingly unstable environments.

My criticism is not that these elements are unexplained. It is that their growing prominence sometimes weakens the aspects of the game I found most interesting.

I was fascinated by the political structure of the Eusan Nation. I wanted to understand more about the Replikas, their relationships, and the society that produced them. I wanted to see how this civilisation functioned beyond the collapsing walls of Sierpinski.

Instead, the narrative increasingly asks me to contemplate the nature of reality itself.

And I am not convinced that this is an inherently more interesting question.

There appears to be an assumption in some discussions of psychological horror that ambiguity automatically makes a story more profound. That the less confidently an event can be explained, the more intellectually rewarding it must be.

I disagree.

Mystery is valuable when it encourages meaningful interpretation. But the presence of multiple possible explanations does not, by itself, make a narrative more satisfying.

Sometimes a corridor is more interesting when you know who lived there than when you are wondering whether the corridor ever existed. Sometimes the horror of a society systematically exploiting artificial people is more compelling than the discovery that reality is being distorted by an unknowable force.

And sometimes I would rather learn more about the miserable lives of ordinary Replikas than spend another hour interpreting the symbolic significance of a fleshy hole in the ground.

Perhaps that makes me insufficiently appreciative of cosmic horror. So be it.

The irony is that I actually enjoy SIGNALIS's surreal imagery on a visual and atmospheric level. Its use of colour, sound, fragmented imagery, and increasingly impossible spaces creates some genuinely memorable sequences.

But atmosphere and narrative satisfaction are not necessarily the same thing. I can appreciate the artistry of an eldritch nightmare while simultaneously wishing the game had spent a little more time exploring the dystopian civilisation surrounding it.

**SIGNALIS is at its most terrifying when its world makes perfect sense, and occasionally at its least satisfying when it stops trying to.**

## The Promise Is the Point

This brings me to perhaps my most controversial opinion about SIGNALIS.

I do not consider its secret ending the most satisfying conclusion. And I am perfectly comfortable arguing that Promise is better.

Not more canonical. Not necessarily more optimistic. Simply better at concluding the particular story that made me care about the game.

SIGNALIS features three standard endings — Leave, Memory, and Promise — alongside the secret Artifact ending. The developers have stated that these endings are not intended to form a conventional hierarchy of good, bad, and true outcomes.

I respect that decision. But artistic intention does not oblige me to find every outcome equally effective.

In **Leave**, Elster reaches the end of her journey but cannot bring herself to confront Ariane. She abandons the final encounter, eventually collapsing and dying alone. There is something deeply tragic about this conclusion. After everything she has endured, Elster is ultimately defeated not by the creatures pursuing her, but by the emotional consequences of fulfilling her objective.

In **Memory**, Elster reaches Ariane, only to discover that the woman she loves no longer recognises her. The reunion takes place, but the relationship that gave it meaning appears to have been lost. Elster dies beside her, leaving the promise unfulfilled.

I find this ending particularly cruel because it denies the characters even the consolation of mutual recognition. They are physically reunited, but the emotional connection that motivated the journey is no longer available to them.

Both endings are thematically appropriate. They explore failure, exhaustion, and the possibility that devotion alone cannot overcome every form of loss. But neither gives me what I wanted from the conclusion.

Then there is **Artifact**.

The secret ending depicts Elster participating in a mysterious ritual involving white lilies, followed by a sequence in which she and Ariane dance together beneath the enormous red eye.

It is a beautiful scene. And I understand why so many players find it moving. After enduring so much suffering, the possibility of seeing Elster and Ariane together again is undeniably appealing.

But what exactly has been resolved?

Are they reunited in reality? Has the cycle ended? Are we witnessing a dream, a memory, an altered state of existence, or something entirely beyond ordinary interpretation?

Perhaps the ambiguity is intentional. Perhaps the ritual represents some form of transcendence that cannot be communicated through the game's more conventional narrative language. Perhaps it offers a genuine escape from the cycle of suffering.

All reasonable interpretations.

None of them gave me the emotional satisfaction I was looking for.

And here is where I feel the need to be deliberately provocative.

**I do not believe that an ending becomes better simply because it requires more interpretation to understand.**

There is a difference between leaving the player with meaningful questions and withholding the conclusion to a story whose emotional stakes have already been thoroughly established.

Artifact may be the most mysterious ending. It may be the most symbolically ambitious. But its beauty does not automatically make it the most satisfying.

For me, that distinction belongs to **Promise**.

Elster finally reaches Ariane. This time, Ariane remembers her. The promise that has motivated the entire journey is finally brought into focus. And Elster, despite her hesitation, ultimately fulfils it.

She ends Ariane's suffering, shares one final moment with her, and dies beside the woman she loves.

It is devastating. And I think it is magnificent.

Not because the characters deserve to die. Not because their relationship is somehow more meaningful when it ends tragically. But because the conclusion finally honours the emotional responsibility at the centre of their story.

Throughout SIGNALIS, the player is driven forward by a promise whose precise meaning remains uncertain. Elster searches, suffers, dies, and returns, repeatedly compelled toward something she cannot abandon.

In Promise, that determination finally achieves something. The journey has an outcome. The relationship reaches a conclusion. And the obligation that defined Elster's existence is fulfilled.

Whether this definitively breaks the larger cycle is another question. The game does not provide absolute certainty, and I am not particularly interested in pretending otherwise.

But I do not need confirmation that every metaphysical problem in SIGNALIS has been solved. I need the emotional story to reach its conclusion.

And Promise provides exactly that.

It is the difference between narrative closure and cosmological closure. One concerns the characters whose suffering I have spent the entire game witnessing. The other concerns the mechanics of a reality the game has deliberately made impossible to understand completely.

I know which matters more to me.

There is also something particularly meaningful about this conclusion when considered alongside the game's treatment of artificial humanity.

Elster's final act is not motivated by obedience to the Eusan Nation, even though the Penrose Program's instructions contemplated precisely this sort of death. She is not merely carrying out a prescribed function. She is honouring a personal commitment made within a relationship that the state never wanted her to develop.

Her actions are defined by Ariane's wishes and their shared history, not by the expectations of her manufacturer.

This is what elevates Promise above a simple tragic ending.

Elster was manufactured to serve. She ultimately chooses whom she serves, why she does so, and what her promise means.

It is a terrible expression of freedom. But freedom nonetheless.

And if the alternative is another ambiguous reunion whose meaning must be reconstructed through an elaborate collection of theories, then I will happily take the ending that allows the characters to rest.

Sometimes, letting go is more meaningful than finding another way to continue.

## The World That Made Me Return

Despite my criticism of its more abstract storytelling, SIGNALIS managed something that relatively few games accomplish.

It made me genuinely excited to replay it.

Not simply because I wanted to discover alternative endings, although that certainly contributed. I wanted to revisit its world.

And replaying SIGNALIS with a better understanding of its story changes the atmosphere of almost every location. The propaganda posters become more sinister once the full implications of the Nation's ideology are understood. Administrative documents that initially appear unremarkable become evidence of a system that has normalised cruelty. The private quarters of Replikas acquire an entirely different emotional weight. And interactions that seemed like incidental encounters become small tragedies.

Even Adler becomes more interesting. What initially looks like the behaviour of a needlessly hostile administrator can be reconsidered through the knowledge that he may have already endured countless failed attempts to prevent the same catastrophe.

I would still very much appreciate it if he stopped trying to murder me. But at least I understand why he is upset.

There is something particularly impressive about a game that can make the player feel differently about an environment without substantially changing the environment itself.

A room that once represented another puzzle to solve becomes someone's home. A document that once seemed like background information becomes an indication of how a particular individual lived. A surviving Replika becomes more than a temporary source of dialogue. And a corpse becomes more than another obstacle in a horror game.

That is what makes SIGNALIS's environmental storytelling so effective. Its world is not simply filled with details. Those details become more meaningful as the player develops a greater understanding of the people who inhabited it.

And, ironically, this is also why I remain somewhat disappointed by the game's reluctance to remain grounded.

I wanted more of this society. More of its artificial inhabitants. More of their private lives, personal contradictions, and quiet acts of individuality. I wanted to understand how these people continued finding reasons to live under a system that regarded them as disposable.

And perhaps that desire is itself evidence of how successfully SIGNALIS constructs its world.

It left me wishing there were more of it.

## Conclusion — Remember Our Promise

SIGNALIS is an extraordinary experience, even if I do not entirely agree with its narrative priorities.

Its dystopian setting is fascinating. Its environmental storytelling is exceptional. Its treatment of artificial consciousness raises questions that extend far beyond the boundaries of its fictional universe. And its central romance is considerably more affecting than its limited presentation might initially suggest.

I particularly appreciate how confidently the game allows its smaller human moments to exist alongside its larger narrative. A frightened Replika, a personal diary, an abandoned bedroom, or two women dancing together aboard a doomed spacecraft can communicate more emotion than an entire sequence of supernatural revelations.

That, ultimately, is what I will remember most fondly.

Not the ritual. Not the cosmic entity. Not the endless uncertainty surrounding the exact nature of reality.

But the people.

The artificial workers who developed friendships, experienced loss, and attempted to preserve some measure of individuality within a society that denied them the right to possess it. The administrator who repeatedly fought against the collapse of the only world he understood. The lonely woman who found companionship in someone she was explicitly instructed not to befriend.

And the Replika who came to love her.

I do not think SIGNALIS needs to prove that these characters are human. Their experiences already make the distinction seem increasingly irrelevant.

And perhaps that is the game's most compelling contradiction.

The Eusan Nation created artificial people in pursuit of a perfectly controlled society. But the humanity of its creations was never something it could entirely suppress.

They developed relationships. They formed memories. They suffered. They loved.

And in the end, their supposedly artificial emotions became more meaningful than the ideological purpose for which they had been manufactured.

I may not appreciate every supernatural direction the game takes, and I certainly do not consider ambiguity an adequate substitute for an emotionally satisfying conclusion. But I can forgive those frustrations when the underlying experience is this memorable.

SIGNALIS is beautifully melancholic, endlessly atmospheric, and populated by characters whose humanity becomes increasingly difficult to deny.

It is a story about devotion in a world that treats individuals as replaceable. About the terrible cost of remembering. And about the possibility that the most human thing a manufactured being can do is make a choice that has nothing to do with the reason it was created.

For me, that choice culminates in Promise.

Not because it is canonically correct. Not because it provides a happy ending. But because it allows a story defined by suffering, devotion, and an impossible obligation to finally come to rest.

**An incredibly enjoyable experience, made all the more memorable by a world I desperately wanted to understand and characters I desperately wanted to see survive.**

And if you ask me, the true tragedy of SIGNALIS is not that its artificial inhabitants became human.

It is that they were human enough to suffer, but never human enough for their society to care.

At least Elster and Ariane had each other.

And yes, I would absolutely go through all of it again for the yuri.
